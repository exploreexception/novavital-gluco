What this means:

NovaVital Gluco is a commercial name for glucose monitoring and is not a therapeutic intervention. The traditional blood glucose metre is an electronic medical device used to assess the concentration of glucose in a blood sample. The metre usually takes disposable test strips and, depending on the system, a lancing device and lancets to collect a tiny capillary blood sample from a fingertip.

The blood glucose metre displays a number that can help someone see what his or her blood glucose is at a certain time. People with diabetes or other needs related to glucose management typically have home metres, but the appropriate testing schedule depends on the individual medical circumstances. How It Works

Most portable blood glucose metres need a test strip with a chemically treated area that reacts with glucose in a blood sample. A small amount of blood is placed on the strip and an electrochemical or similar sensing system generates a signal related to the glucose concentration. The metre captures that signal and returns an answer, usually in the most common units in the United States.

The accuracy of a reading is not limited to the electronics inside the metre. Test strips, blood-sampling technique, storage conditions, expiration status, environmental conditions and user technique can influence results. Therefore, cleaning and drying the hands before testing, using an adequate sample and following the manufacturer’s instructions are important considerations of reliable home monitoring.

Some professional Nova glucose monitoring systems depend on specific specimen and quality control procedures. By default those attributes should not be extended to a consumer device called NovaVital Gluco. The exact manual for the model in use should be followed for specimen type, operating temperature, strip requirements, calibration procedures and error messages.

Key Benefits

The primary potential benefit of a blood glucose metre is that it offers a current glucose measurement without requiring every test to be conducted in a laboratory. If you have been instructed to check your glucose at home, regular readings can provide information about how glucose fluctuates during the day and how readings are related to meals, activity, medication, illness or other situations.

Regular monitoring also may be easier to manage with a portable system. Modern metres keep past results and some systems include features for the handling or transfer of readings. However, the value of these features is model dependent and stored numbers are only as useful as the testing technique and interpretation supporting them.

Home monitoring can make it easier to communicate with your health care professional by maintaining a record of readings over time. This is not a replacement for clinical assessment, laboratory testing or professional advice. A single metre result may also be misleading if the sample is contaminated or the strip is damaged or the result is affected by a known interference.

Another potential benefit is that glucose testing can reveal trends that might be difficult to pick up otherwise. But at the same time, too much testing, or taking individual readings out of context, can bring about needless worry. The frequency and timing of testing should be suited to a person’s treatment plan, not the ability of the metre to provide a reading.
