What it is:

NovaVital Gluco is a commercial name for glucose monitoring and is not a therapeutic intervention. The traditional blood glucose metre is an electronic testing device used to measure the concentration of glucose in a blood sample. The metre usually accepts disposable test strips and, depending on the system, a lancing device and lancets to take a tiny capillary blood sample from a fingertip.

The blood glucose metre displays a number that can help a person understand what his or her blood glucose is at a certain time. People with diabetes or other needs related to glucose management typically have home metres, but the appropriate testing schedule depends on the individual medical circumstances. A metre alone does not confirm all types of diabetes or the cause of an abnormal glucose result.

How Does It Work

Most handheld blood glucose metres depend on a test strip with a chemically treated area that engages with glucose in a blood sample. A small amount of blood is applied on the strip and an electrochemical or similar sensing system creates a signal related to the glucose concentration. The metre reads that signal and gives an answer, usually in the usual measurement units in the United States.

The accuracy of a reading is dependent on more than the electronics inside the metre. Test strips, blood-sampling technique, storage conditions, expiration status, environmental conditions and user technique can alter results. Therefore, carefully washing and drying the hands before testing, using an adequate sample and following the manufacturer’s instructions are essential steps of reliable home monitoring.

Some professional Nova glucose monitoring systems depend on specific specimen and quality control procedures. By default those attributes should not be applied to a consumer device called NovaVital Gluco. The exact manual for the model in use should be examined for specimen type, operating temperature, strip requirements, calibration procedures and error messages.

Potential Benefits

The primary potential benefit of a blood glucose metre is that it delivers a current glucose measurement without requiring every test to be completed in a laboratory. If you have been instructed to check your glucose at home, regular readings can help reveal patterns about how glucose fluctuates during the day and how readings are related to meals, activity, medication, illness or other situations.

Ongoing monitoring also may be more manageable with a portable system. Modern metres retain past results and some systems feature features for the arrangement or transfer of readings. However, the value of these features is specific to each model and stored numbers are only as useful as the testing technique and interpretation applied to them.

Home monitoring can assist you in communicating with your health care professional by giving a record of readings over time. This is not a stand-in for clinical assessment, laboratory testing or professional advice. A single metre result may also be inaccurate if the sample is contaminated or the strip is damaged or the result is affected by a known interference.

Another potential benefit is that glucose testing can assist in detecting trends that might be challenging to detect otherwise. But at the same time, too much testing, or taking individual readings out of context, can result in needless worry. The frequency and timing of testing should be consistent to a person’s treatment plan, not the ability of the metre to generate a reading.

Blood glucose metres are useful monitoring devices, but are not laboratory analysers. The results could be variable from laboratory measurements within the instrument accuracy limits. The management of test strips is especially important.
