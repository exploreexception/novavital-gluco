How Does It Operate

Most handheld blood glucose metres depend on a test strip with a chemically treated area that interacts with glucose in a blood sample. A small amount of blood is added on the strip and an electrochemical or similar sensing system generates a signal related to the glucose concentration. The metre gets that signal and provides an answer, usually in the widely used units in the United States.

The accuracy of a reading is more than the electronics inside the metre. Test strips, blood-sampling technique, storage conditions, expiration status, environmental conditions and user technique can affect results. Therefore, proper hand washing and drying before testing, using an adequate sample and following the manufacturer’s instructions are important considerations of reliable home monitoring.

Some professional Nova glucose monitoring systems use specific specimen and quality control procedures. By default those attributes should not be carried over to a consumer device called NovaVital Gluco. The exact manual for the model in use should be followed for specimen type, operating temperature, strip requirements, calibration procedures and error messages.

Key Benefits

The primary potential benefit of a blood glucose metre is that it supplies a current glucose measurement without requiring every test to be performed in a laboratory. If you have been instructed to check your glucose at home, regular readings can offer insight about how glucose fluctuates during the day and how readings are related to meals, activity, medication, illness or other situations.

Frequent monitoring also may be more manageable with a portable system. Modern metres save past results and some systems feature features for the handling or transfer of readings. However, the value of these features is specific to each model and stored numbers are only as useful as the testing technique and interpretation behind them.

Home monitoring can support communication with your health care professional by giving a record of readings over time. This is not a alternative for clinical assessment, laboratory testing or professional advice. A single metre result may also be misinterpreted if the sample is contaminated or the strip is damaged or the result is affected by a known interference.

Another potential benefit is that glucose testing can reveal trends that might be challenging to detect otherwise. But at the same time, too much testing, or taking individual readings out of context, can create needless worry. The frequency and timing of testing should be proper to a person’s treatment plan, not the ability of the metre to deliver a reading.

Blood glucose metres are useful monitoring devices, but are not laboratory analysers. The results could be different from laboratory measurements within the instrument accuracy limits. The maintenance of test strips is especially significant. There is also a practical risk with repeated finger stick testing such as soreness, minor bleeding, bruising or skin irritation. More importantly, if a wrong glucose result is given it could cause someone to make an improper treatment decision.
