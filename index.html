How It Works

Most portable blood glucose metres use a test strip with a chemically treated area that engages with glucose in a blood sample. A small amount of blood is deposited on the strip and an electrochemical or similar sensing system records a signal related to the glucose concentration. The metre receives that signal and provides an answer, usually in the standard units in the United States.

The accuracy of a reading is influenced by more than the electronics inside the metre. Test strips, blood-sampling technique, storage conditions, expiration status, environmental conditions and user technique can alter results. Therefore, washing then drying the hands before testing, using an adequate sample and following the manufacturer’s instructions are important elements of reliable home monitoring.

Some professional Nova glucose monitoring systems need specific specimen and quality control procedures. By default those attributes should not be propagated to a consumer device called NovaVital Gluco. The exact manual for the model in use should be examined for specimen type, operating temperature, strip requirements, calibration procedures and error messages.

Potential Benefits

The primary potential benefit of a blood glucose metre is that it supplies a current glucose measurement without requiring every test to be done in a laboratory. If you have been instructed to check your glucose at home, regular readings can give useful information about how glucose fluctuates during the day and how readings are related to meals, activity, medication, illness or other situations.

Ongoing monitoring also may be more practical with a portable system. Modern metres store past results and some systems include features for the management or transfer of readings. However, the value of these features is specific to each model and stored numbers are only as useful as the testing technique and interpretation behind them.

Home monitoring can aid communication with your health care professional by giving a record of readings over time. This is not a stand-in for clinical assessment, laboratory testing or professional advice. A single metre result may also be inaccurate if the sample is contaminated or the strip is damaged or the result is affected by a known interference.

Another potential benefit is that glucose testing can assist in detecting trends that might be difficult to pick up otherwise. But at the same time, too much testing, or taking individual readings out of context, can create needless worry. The frequency and timing of testing should be fitting to a person’s treatment plan, not the ability of the metre to generate a reading.
