How Does It Function

Most handheld blood glucose metres depend on a test strip with a chemically treated area that engages with glucose in a blood sample. A small amount of blood is deposited on the strip and an electrochemical or similar sensing system delivers a signal related to the glucose concentration. The metre detects that signal and returns an answer, usually in the most common units in the United States.

The accuracy of a reading is not limited to the electronics inside the metre. Test strips, blood-sampling technique, storage conditions, expiration status, environmental conditions and user technique can affect results. Therefore, washing then drying the hands before testing, using an adequate sample and following the manufacturer’s instructions are essential steps of reliable home monitoring.

Some professional Nova glucose monitoring systems depend on specific specimen and quality control procedures. Potential Benefits

The primary potential benefit of a blood glucose metre is that it gives a current glucose measurement without requiring every test to be completed in a laboratory. If you have been instructed to check your glucose at home, regular readings can offer insight about how glucose fluctuates during the day and how readings are related to meals, activity, medication, illness or other situations.

Routine monitoring also may be more convenient with a portable system. Modern metres retain past results and some systems add features for the handling or transfer of readings. However, the value of these features is model dependent and stored numbers are only as useful as the testing technique and interpretation used with them.

Home monitoring can assist you in communicating with your health care professional by offering a record of readings over time. This is not a replacement for clinical assessment, laboratory testing or professional advice. A single metre result may also be misinterpreted if the sample is contaminated or the strip is damaged or the result is affected by a known interference.

Another potential benefit is that glucose testing can reveal trends that might be challenging to detect otherwise. But at the same time, too much testing, or taking individual readings out of context, can result in needless worry. The frequency and timing of testing should be proper to a person’s treatment plan, not the ability of the metre to provide a reading.
