How It Works

Most compact blood glucose metres require a test strip with a chemically treated area that responds with glucose in a blood sample. A small amount of blood is added on the strip and an electrochemical or similar sensing system creates a signal related to the glucose concentration. The metre gets that signal and returns an answer, usually in the usual measurement units in the United States.

The accuracy of a reading is more than the electronics inside the metre. Test strips, blood-sampling technique, storage conditions, expiration status, environmental conditions and user technique can impact results. Therefore, carefully washing and drying the hands before testing, using an adequate sample and following the manufacturer’s instructions are essential steps of reliable home monitoring.

Some professional Nova glucose monitoring systems depend on specific specimen and quality control procedures. By default those attributes should not be applied to a consumer device called NovaVital Gluco. The exact manual for the model in use should be checked for specimen type, operating temperature, strip requirements, calibration procedures and error messages.

Key Benefits

The primary potential benefit of a blood glucose metre is that it offers a current glucose measurement without requiring every test to be performed in a laboratory. If you have been instructed to check your glucose at home, regular readings can provide information about how glucose changes during the day and how readings are related to meals, activity, medication, illness or other situations.

Ongoing monitoring also may be easier to manage with a portable system. Modern metres retain past results and some systems include features for the organization or transfer of readings. However, the value of these features is determined by the model and stored numbers are only as useful as the testing technique and interpretation applied to them.

Home monitoring can help you communicate with your health care professional by supplying a record of readings over time. This is not a substitute for clinical assessment, laboratory testing or professional advice. A single metre result may also be deceptive if the sample is contaminated or the strip is damaged or the result is affected by a known interference.

Another potential benefit is that glucose testing can help detect trends that might be hard to notice otherwise. But at the same time, too much testing, or taking individual readings out of context, can result in needless worry. The frequency and timing of testing should be suited to a person’s treatment plan, not the ability of the metre to generate a reading.
